# Research Dossier: kalshi-adaptive-loop — concept

**Slug:** kalshi-adaptive-loop
**Phase:** concept
**Timestamp:** 20260407T062000Z
**Depth:** deep (forced by complexity: complex)

---

## Summary

An adaptive forecasting loop is feasible but faces a hard statistical constraint: at $10/day (~2-3 bets), you need ~70-100 days to accumulate the 200 predictions required to reliably detect calibration improvement (Brier Δ of 0.03 at 80% power). This means the first adaptation cycle won't produce statistically significant signal for ~3 months. The architecture should use DSPy-style metric-driven prompt optimization where resolved Kalshi bets serve as the labeled dataset, with a hybrid pipeline: universal calibration/update logic + per-domain retrieval modules. Surface expansion should follow: weather daily → econ monthlies → crypto → finance → politics, gated by a 50-prediction baseline on each surface.

---

## Findings

- **Superforecasters improve ~15-20% in Brier score across tournament years** (Tetlock 2015). Improvement comes from: granular probability updates, update frequency, structured post-mortems, and base-rate anchoring. Improvement plateaus after ~2 years.
- **No production automated recalibration system exists.** Metaculus, GJP, and Hypermind all use human-in-the-loop improvement. Automated prompt/pipeline adaptation for forecasting is an open research problem — this system would be novel.
- **DSPy's MIPROv2 is the most applicable optimization mechanism.** It jointly optimizes instructions and few-shot examples using Bayesian optimization, scored against a metric function. Resolved Kalshi bets are the labeled dataset; Brier score is the fitness function. Requires hundreds of resolved predictions minimum.
- **PromptBreeder (evolutionary prompt mutation) is compute-heavy and demonstrated only on classification tasks.** Self-refine works within-generation but doesn't learn across episodes without external memory. Neither is directly applicable without significant adaptation.
- **73 paired predictions needed to detect Brier Δ of 0.05** (paired t-test, 80% power). For Δ of 0.03 (more realistic improvement): ~200 predictions needed. Sequential analysis (SPRT) can cut sample requirements by 40-60%.
- **200 predictions is the practical sweet spot** for reliable calibration evaluation. At that point you can run 5-bin calibration curves (20-25 per bin) and detect moderate improvements.
- **Calibration transfers almost perfectly across domains; resolution does not.** GJP data shows ~60% of forecasting skill is domain-general (calibration discipline, base-rate reasoning, update frequency), ~40% is domain-specific knowledge. LLMs show the same pattern.
- **Cold-start on new domains: ~50-100 resolved questions** before calibration stabilizes. Superforecasters reach baseline faster (~30) due to domain-general skills providing a floor.
- **Weather daily markets are the optimal first surface:** fast feedback, unambiguous resolution (NOAA data), least efficient (retail-heavy, poor model adoption), buildable models (GFS/ECMWF freely available).
- **Economics (CPI, jobs, Fed) are the natural second surface:** monthly cadence, very clean resolution, high liquidity, but most efficient market (professional traders with Bloomberg terminals).
- **Crypto daily/weekly is third:** fast feedback, liquidity, persistent inefficiency around round-number thresholds, but noisier.
- **Politics should be last:** long cycles, ambiguous resolution risk, narrative-driven, hardest to backtest.

## Trade-offs

### Option: DSPy-style metric-driven optimization
**Pros:** Directly uses resolved bets as labeled data. Principled (Bayesian optimization). Can optimize both instructions and few-shot examples. Well-documented framework.
**Cons:** Needs hundreds of resolved predictions before meaningful optimization. Compute cost of running optimization sweeps. Requires backtesting infrastructure.
**Verdict:** selected — best fit for the constraint set

### Option: Evolutionary prompt mutation (PromptBreeder)
**Pros:** More exploratory — can discover novel prompt strategies. Doesn't require labeled data upfront.
**Cons:** Compute-heavy. Only demonstrated on classification. Population maintenance is complex. Hard to constrain to forecasting-relevant mutations.
**Verdict:** rejected — too expensive and unproven for this domain

### Option: Manual adaptation from post-mortem analysis
**Pros:** Simplest to implement. Human judgment on what to change. No infrastructure needed.
**Cons:** Doesn't scale. Human bottleneck. Subjective decisions about what to change. Can't A/B test effectively.
**Verdict:** selected as bootstrap mechanism — use manual adaptation for the first 200 predictions, then switch to automated optimization once the labeled dataset is large enough

### Option: Universal pipeline vs per-domain pipelines
**Pros (universal):** Simpler architecture. Calibration skills transfer. One thing to optimize.
**Cons (universal):** Domain-specific knowledge can't be captured. Resolution accuracy varies by domain.
**Pros (per-domain):** Tailored retrieval and reference classes. Better resolution.
**Cons (per-domain):** N pipelines to maintain. Cold-start on each new domain.
**Verdict:** hybrid — universal calibration/update pipeline core + pluggable per-domain retrieval/knowledge modules

### Option: A/B testing vs sequential comparison for measuring adaptation
**Pros (A/B):** Gold standard. Controls for question difficulty. Directly comparable.
**Cons (A/B):** Requires 2x predictions per question (expensive at $10/day). Halves effective sample size.
**Pros (sequential):** Full budget on production pipeline. Simpler.
**Cons (sequential):** Confounded by question difficulty changes over time. Less rigorous.
**Verdict:** sequential with CUSUM charts — use cumulative Brier score difference tracking. A/B testing deferred until budget allows or as periodic spot-checks.

## Recommendations

- **Bootstrap phase (predictions 1-200): manual adaptation from post-mortem analysis.** Use the F1-F10 failure taxonomy from kalshi-calibration-harness to identify the weakest pipeline phase every 50 predictions. Make one targeted change per batch. Track before/after with CUSUM charts.
- **Optimization phase (predictions 200+): DSPy-style automated optimization.** Treat the 200+ resolved bets as the labeled dataset. Optimize research prompts and calibration instructions using MIPROv2 with Brier score as the fitness function.
- **Use sequential analysis (mSPRT) for continuous monitoring** rather than waiting for fixed batch sizes. This lets you detect large improvements early while still accumulating data for subtle effects.
- **Hybrid pipeline architecture: universal core + domain modules.** One calibration/update pipeline. Per-domain: retrieval sources, reference class databases, and domain-specific few-shot examples.
- **Surface expansion ladder:** Weather daily (current) → Econ monthlies (after 50 weather predictions) → Crypto daily (after 50 econ) → Finance weekly → Politics. Gate: 50-prediction baseline on current surface before expanding. Never abandon a surface — keep parallel streams.
- **Version every pipeline change as a git commit** with the batch number, failure pattern that triggered it, and the specific modification. This creates a traceable adaptation history.
- **Expect ~3 months to first statistically significant signal** at $10/day. Set expectations accordingly — early batches are about data collection, not measurable improvement.

## Adjacent Findings

- **Compute budget for optimization is non-trivial:** DSPy MIPROv2 optimization runs evaluate dozens of prompt candidates against the full validation set. At 200+ resolved predictions, each optimization run costs ~$5-15 in API calls (Claude/GPT-4 inference). At monthly optimization cadence, this adds ~$60-180/year on top of the $3,600/year bet budget. This matters because the total system cost is higher than just the betting budget — factor optimization compute into the operating budget. Source: DSPy documentation and benchmark costs.
- **Economics markets are dominated by professionals with rate-model infrastructure:** Edge in econ markets is structurally harder to find than in weather because the competition is institutional traders, not retail. This matters because the surface expansion ladder should set different edge thresholds per domain — a 5% edge in weather markets is achievable, but the same threshold in econ markets may produce zero qualifying bets. Source: Kalshi market efficiency analysis.
- **Calibration improvement plateaus after ~2 years in human forecasters:** Tetlock's longitudinal data shows diminishing returns on calibration improvement. If the same pattern holds for LLM-based systems, the system should have a "graduation" criterion — when improvement rate drops below a threshold, shift focus from calibration to resolution (domain knowledge depth) or expand to harder surfaces. Source: Tetlock, "Superforecasting" (2015), longitudinal tournament data.

## Open Questions

- What is the compute cost of running DSPy MIPROv2 optimization on the resolved prediction dataset? Need to prototype before committing to automated optimization.
- Should the system maintain a "prompt changelog" — a structured log of every prompt/strategy change with the triggering failure pattern and outcome?
- How to handle the transition from bootstrap (manual) to optimization (automated) — hard cutover at 200 predictions, or gradual blend?
- What edge threshold should vary by surface? Weather might use 5%, econ might need 10%+ to overcome market efficiency.
- How to detect when calibration improvement has plateaued and shift strategy?
- Should the system bet on the same question with both old and new pipelines as periodic A/B spot-checks (costs 2x on those questions)?
